## What is it?

NASA Lunabotics asks teams to solve a serious robotics problem: load, haul, and dump lunar simulant autonomously, using LiDAR sensing, reliable avionics, and mechanical systems tough enough for a dusty, hostile environment. And do it better than 49 other teams.

## What did I do?

- Painstakingly optimized the mechanical architecture for excavation, storage, deposition, mobility, sensing, and electronics. Many late nights in SolidWorks.
- Built a lunar-style arena from scratch and wrote countless test plans for physical validation. The arena cost $10,000 and took a month of manual labor in the bitter Pittsburgh winter.
- Kept mechanical, avionics, software, and systems work aligned so the rover functioned once integrated—and so testing and subteam timelines actually coalesced.
- Presented progress to reviewers from NASA, Blue Origin, Astrobotic, and Tesla. We earned NASA’s **Best Presentation by a First-Year Team** award.
- Served as the primary architect of our SRR, PDR, CDR, and Robot Acceptance Review.
- Handled a smattering of other matters involving electronics, actuator tuning, path planning, competition strategy, logistics, and politics, to name a few.

## How did it turn out?

Our rover performed well and scored points for autonomy, but fell short of the podium against more experienced teams. Nevertheless, the year was a massive success for a first-year team. We built, from the inside out, an efficient 80 kg mechatronic system designed to carry a substantial payload and traverse powdery lunar simulant while managing slip.

The designs, review structure, interfaces, and tests that my team and I established are being used for this year’s robot, which I’m confident has a shot at gold. It is not always the person who lights the torch who carries it across the finish line—but it damn well better be those they pass it to.
